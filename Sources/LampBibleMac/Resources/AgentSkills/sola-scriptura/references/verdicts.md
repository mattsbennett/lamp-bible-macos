# Verdicts and load

Two axes. The verdict says what the claim rests on. The load says how much falls
over if it goes. You need both: the verdict tells the writer what is wrong, the
load tells them whether to care.

## The verdict ladder

**Stated.** Scripture asserts it, and the quoted text carries it on its own. Not
reported — except in one case: the claim is true and stated *somewhere else* than
where the writing cites it. That is still a finding, because the citation is doing
no work and the next reader to check it will not find it.

**Entailed.** It follows from stated texts by an argument you can write out in a
line or two, adding no premise from outside scripture. Not reported, unless the
writing presents it as stated and the step is one a reader would not take on their
own — then the remedy is to show the step.

**Inferred.** The text permits it. The text also permits something else. Reported
when the writing gives no sign that a reading has been chosen. The remedy is
almost always to mark it, not to drop it: *I take this to mean*, *the most natural
reading is*, *Matthew seems to be drawing*. One phrase converts an overreach into
an honest argument, and the writer keeps their point.

**Imported.** A premise comes from outside scripture: a creed, a confession, a
commentator, a lexicon, received practice, scholarly consensus, historical or
cultural background, or a religious idea so common it feels scriptural. Always
reported. The remedy is to name the source or to drop the load — and naming it is
usually enough. A piece may say *Bruce argues* and stand; it may not say *scripture
teaches* when Bruce is the one who taught it.

**Unsupported.** No text establishes it and none is offered. Reported. Distinguish
this from imported: unsupported has no visible source at all, which often means the
writer absorbed it and will be glad to know.

**Contrary.** The cited text, or the one that governs the question, tells against
the claim. Reported first, whatever its load. Quote the text and stop; do not argue
the doctrine.

## Telling entailed from inferred

This is the judgment the whole audit turns on, and there is a mechanical test.

**Write the argument out as premises. Then look for the premise you added.**

If every premise is a text you can quote, it is entailed. If one premise is
something you know rather than something you read, that premise is the finding —
and naming it is the most useful sentence you will write. "This needs the further
premise that the breaking of bread in Acts 20:7 was weekly rather than occasional,
which the verse does not say" is worth more to a writer than any verdict label.

Two habits that corrupt this test:

- **Collapsing steps.** Three inferences stacked read as one entailment, because
  each step felt small. Write all three out. A chain is no stronger than its
  weakest link, and the link is usually the second.
- **Letting familiarity stand in for evidence.** A conclusion you have heard since
  childhood feels entailed. Ask what a reader who had never heard it would need.

## The load axis

**Load-bearing.** It is the conclusion, or a step the conclusion needs. Remove it
and the piece has not made its case.

**Supporting.** It strengthens a point that survives without it. Remove it and the
argument stands, slightly thinner.

**Decorative.** Colour, illustration, an aside, a turn of phrase. Remove it and
nothing changes but the texture.

Judge load from the chain you wrote in step 1, not from how emphatic the sentence
is. Writers often state their load-bearing premises quietly and their decorations
with force.

## The grid

| | Load-bearing | Supporting | Decorative |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contrary** | Report first | Report | Report briefly |
| **Unsupported** | Report | Report | Minor list |
| **Imported** | Report | Report | Minor list |
| **Inferred** | Report | Report if it changes the conclusion | Leave it |
| **Entailed** | Report only if the step is hidden | Leave it | Leave it |
| **Stated** | Only if cited to the wrong place | Same | Same |

"Leave it" means leave it. Writing is allowed decoration, and an audit that flags a
figure of speech loses the authority it needs for the finding that matters.
