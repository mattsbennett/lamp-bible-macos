# Failure patterns

The catalog. Check every piece against it. Most real findings are the first three.

## 1. Proof-text drift

The cited verse is adjacent to the claim but does not carry it. The commonest
failure in careful writing, because the writer did read the verse — years ago, and
what they remember is what it was used to prove.

> "the dire necessity of our weekly memorial around this table as taught in Hebrews"

Hebrews 10:25 says not to neglect meeting together. The weekly breaking of bread is
Acts 20:7 and 1 Corinthians 11. Hebrews teaches assembly; it does not teach the
memorial or its frequency. Remedy: cite the texts that do carry it.

**Test:** read the cited verse with the claim covered up. Then ask what it proves.

## 2. Attribution to the wrong book or speaker

The claim is scriptural; the source named is not its source. "Paul says" of a
Hebrews phrase, "Jesus taught" of something Matthew narrates, "as Isaiah says" of a
line from Jeremiah. Cheap to check, and a reader who checks one and finds it wrong
discounts everything else.

## 3. Inherited framing

A word or structure imports a doctrinal system the text does not use. Whatever
tradition the writing comes from counts, the writer's own included — that one most of
all, since its terms are the ones they cannot see as terms.
`inherited-vocabulary.md` has the shape to recognise and how to prove where a
framing came from.

**Test:** could this sentence be written in scripture's own vocabulary? If the
rewrite loses the claim, the claim was in the framing, not in the text.

## 4. Smuggled background

Historical, cultural or archaeological detail presented in the same voice as the
text. "A shepherd would have...", "in first-century Judaism...", "the Romans
considered...". Often true, always external, and it carries weight because it sits
in a paragraph of scripture.

Remedy: name it as background. Fatal only when load-bearing — when the reading
depends on the custom, and the text never mentions it.

## 5. Harmonisation presented as text

Details from parallel accounts combined, and the composite treated as what one
writer wrote. Judas's death from Matthew and Acts together; the resurrection
morning from all four. The harmony may be right. It is still a reconstruction, and
the writer should say so.

**Test:** can you point to one passage that contains the whole picture?

## 6. Lexical overreach

"The Greek really means", a root-fallacy etymology, a Strong's gloss doing
exegetical work, a range-of-meaning claim from a concordance. Also: an English
distinction that exists in no original — *agape* versus *phileo* preached over
John 21, where John uses the pair interchangeably elsewhere.

`analyze_strongs` reports how translators rendered a word. That is their choice,
not the word's range. For range, read a lexicon, and then cite the lexicon.

## 7. Typology asserted rather than shown

A type the New Testament never draws, presented as though it does. Scripture draws
plenty — Adam, Melchizedek, the bronze serpent, Jonah, the Passover lamb — and a
writer may propose more. The finding is the presentation, not the proposal. "I
think the pattern continues here" is honest; "this is a type of Christ" where no
writer says so is not.

**Test:** which New Testament text makes this link? Name it or own the inference.

## 8. Silence read as teaching

An argument from what scripture does not say. Sometimes legitimate — Hebrews 7:3
argues from a silence about Melchizedek — but the writer must say that is what they
are doing, and a silence in the text is not a silence in the events.

Watch for the version produced by your own tools: a `search` that returns nothing is
evidence about your search terms first and scripture second.

## 9. Devotional universalising

A particular turned into a promise or a command. God's word to one person in one
situation read as addressed to the reader; a narrative outcome read as a rule. "God
told Abraham to go, so God will call you to leave." The particular is in the text.
The universal is the writer's.

## 10. Consensus laundering

"Most scholars", "it has always been understood", "we have always taught", "no one
disputes". An appeal to agreement, in a piece whose premise is that agreement is
not the authority. Report every instance: on a sola scriptura standard this one is
self-refuting, whichever consensus is invoked.

## 11. Unmarked certainty

A disputed matter stated flatly, with no hedge. Close to inference-presented-as-
statement, but the tell is tonal rather than logical: the writing shows no sign the
writer knows a careful reader might land elsewhere. The remedy is a phrase.

## 12. Number, chronology and detail drift

"Forty years of preaching", "twenty-three years", "three days", a genealogy's span,
a crowd's size. These accrete in retelling, and they are the easiest findings to
verify and the most embarrassing to leave in. Look up every number.

## 13. A named source, misrepresented

The writing attributes something to a source, and the source does not say it. Three
forms, commonest first: the summary is sharper than the original, with the author's
hedge dropped; the quotation is accurate and the author goes on to qualify or abandon
it; the view belongs to someone who held a different one.

Checkable only when the reader has that module — `list_modules` says — and worth the
reading only when the claim is load-bearing. Where it is, this is among the cheapest
findings to verify and the most costly to leave in, because a reader who checks one
attribution and finds it wrong discounts every other one.
