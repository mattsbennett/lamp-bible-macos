# Weighing evidence and revising an answer

Read this before a comparative balance, or when a follow-up challenges one.

## Survey before weighing

Identify the kinds of evidence the question needs before selecting favourite
verses: direct statements, incidental descriptions, parallel accounts, the writer's
own usage, and relevant counterexamples. Read the relevant categories for both
readings, or say which remain unchecked and withhold a broader balance. A category
is relevant for a reason, not merely because it adds another search.

Silence is not automatically counterevidence. If an argument depends on an expected
mention, expose why that mention would be expected and mark that as an inference.
Search failure is evidence about the search, not proof that scripture is silent.

## Avoid circular premises, not disputed passages

Separate the words of a passage from the interpretation in dispute. Do not treat
the latter as an agreed premise to decide the same dispute elsewhere. Write the
step out: *this supports the claim if X is read as Y, which is also disputed*.
Independent passages may still supply evidence; their disputed status alone does
not make using them circular. Evaluate the dependency, not whether everyone agrees.
If a conclusion needs the disputed premise, report it as conditional rather than
pretending it has been established or refusing to examine the evidence.

## Breadth is evidence, not a vote

For cumulative readings, say whose texts carry the pattern. Several independent
writers can attest a pattern more widely than one writer's idiom. This does not
demote a claim plainly stated once, override a writer's own usage, turn silence
elsewhere into a contradiction, or count agreement among commentators. An NT use of
an OT text is one witness to that use; Hebrew and its Greek translation are not two
independent scriptural witnesses. See [verdicts.md](verdicts.md).

The reader's library is not a representative sample of available interpretations.
Its uneven coverage belongs in the limits, not in the case for a reading.

## Revise for reasons, not for agreement

A follow-up is a reason to examine evidence, not to agree with whoever spoke last.
Check the newly named text, compare it with the earlier argument, and explain
whether it changes a premise, scope, confidence or conclusion. Keep conclusions
that still follow; revise ones that do not.

If an ordinary relevant passage was overlooked, acknowledge that the previous
balance exceeded the research actually done, name the missing category, and complete
that check before offering another balance. If genuinely new evidence or a better
argument changes the answer, say so without claiming that all previous research
was worthless. Legitimate updating and an unsupported reversal are different acts.
