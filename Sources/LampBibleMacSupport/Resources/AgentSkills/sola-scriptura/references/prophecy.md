# Prophecy

Read this for a question or claim about prophecy: what a prophecy foretells, how
far it has been fulfilled, the order of the events it describes, or what its
symbols stand for.

The principles below follow Jonathan Burke, *More Sure Word of Prophecy* (2010).
Each one is drawn from the texts cited with it. Those texts, not the book, are the
authority, so look them up before relying on a principle in an answer. Like every
illustration in this skill, the examples show a method; they are not readings to
transfer to another passage unchecked.

## Principles of prophecy

Prophecy both foretells events and forth-tells God's lessons. These principles
concern its foretelling.

1. **God reveals his plans and his future work through prophecy** (Amos 3:7;
   Deuteronomy 29:29).
   - Prophecy is revelation to be understood, not a riddle beyond reading.
   - The revealed things belong to us "that we may do all the words of this law".
     Revelation is given to be read, understood and acted on.
   - Deuteronomy 29:29 also bounds it: the secret things belong to God. A reading
     that claims more than the texts reveal has passed from the revealed things to
     speculation, and that is the finding.
2. **What prophecy reveals will come to pass, unless God says otherwise**
   (Isaiah 55:10–11; Numbers 14:21; Jonah 3:4–10). God's word does not return void,
   and a long delay is not failure.
   - **Some prophecies are unconditional.** All the earth shall be filled with the
     glory of the LORD (Numbers 14:21).
   - **Others are conditional, and the condition may show only on reading on.**
     Jonah 3:4 reads as unconditional. Verses 5–10 show Nineveh's repentance and
     God turning from the evil he had said he would do.
   - **Look beyond the verse for the condition.** Read the passage around a
     prophecy, and the other passages that speak of the same event, before calling
     it either kind.
   - **Two errors are possible, and the same rule settles both.** One is treating a
     conditional prophecy as unconditional because the reading stopped at the
     announcement. The other is treating every prophecy as dependent on human
     response. Either way, the condition, or its absence, must come from scripture.
     Do not conclude that a prophecy will not come to pass without a statement of
     scripture to that effect. A condition supplied to rescue an interpretation is
     an imported premise.
3. **Prophecy follows a chronological order, according to the divine plan**
   (Genesis 18:17; Psalm 25:14; Daniel 9:1–2; Revelation 1:1–2).
   - God does not leave his people uninformed of the work he is doing in their
     generation. In Genesis 18:17 he asks whether he should hide from Abraham
     what he is about to do.
   - He guides those who fear him so that they may take part in his purpose
     (Psalm 25:14).
   - So each generation can learn where it stands in the order. In the first year
     of Darius, Daniel understood from the books the number of years the word to
     Jeremiah gave for Jerusalem's desolations.
   - Revelation was given to show God's servants things which must shortly come to
     pass (Revelation 1:1).

   The order belongs to the plan, not necessarily to the arrangement of a book:
   some prophetic books do not set out their chapters in strict chronological
   succession. So show an order of events from the text's own markers of sequence
   and time, never from where a passage stands in its book. The Olivet prophecy
   has such markers: "but before all these" (Luke 21:12), and "until the times of
   the Gentiles be fulfilled" (Luke 21:24). A timeline assembled across several
   books is a reconstruction: show each step, as for any harmonisation (pattern 5
   in `failure-patterns.md`).
4. **A prophecy may speak of a kind of event repeated over time.** The details
   differ each time, but the same kind of thing comes to pass. Two forms:
   - **A first fulfilment, then a fuller one.** The first, often smaller, is the
     model for a later one that fulfils the description more completely. The
     promise to David's son (2 Samuel 7:12–14) is fulfilled first in Solomon, who
     built the house. It is applied to Christ in Acts 2:30, where Peter says David
     knew it, and in Hebrews 1:5.
   - **A cycle, set off whenever its conditions recur.** Deuteronomy 28's curses
     come "if thou wilt not hearken" (verse 15). So they return each time the
     condition is met, for as long as the covenant that sets it stands.
     - Scripture traces the exile to Assyria to that disobedience (2 Kings
       18:11–12), and Babylon's siege repeats the pattern (2 Kings 25:1).
     - Jeremiah 34:18–21 gives the same reason for Babylon's coming, and
       Jeremiah 28:13–14 takes up Deuteronomy 28:48's yoke of iron.

   One fulfilment does not exhaust such a prophecy, and a nearer fulfilment does
   not rule out a further one. But a second application is almost always expounded
   by scripture itself, so where one is suspected, let scripture guide. Grade a
   fulfilment as a link, with `echoes-and-patterns.md`:
   - a fulfilment a text declares is Drawn;
   - one no text declares is at best Inferred, with its grade;
   - an event after the scriptural record is outside scripture. Its facts are
     history, an external premise to name. Only the correspondence is the
     reading.
5. **A prophecy may be expanded in further detail elsewhere in scripture.** A later
   prophecy can expand an earlier one. Within one book a sequence of visions can
   build on each other, each understood only after the ones before it; Daniel is
   the typical case.
   - Matthew 24:15–16 speaks of the abomination of desolation "spoken of by Daniel
     the prophet" standing in the holy place.
   - Luke 21:20–22 gives the same warning as Jerusalem compassed with armies.

   Read a prophecy with its parallels before deciding what it says. Count the
   parallels as one prophecy expanded, not as independent witnesses to it. Per
   *Breadth of witness* in `verdicts.md`, one saying reported in parallel accounts
   is not several witnesses.

## Symbols in prophecy

Symbols in prophecy are no special code. They are the metaphors and similes
scripture uses everywhere:
- **A metaphor** puts one thing for another: the waters of the river, "even the
  king of Assyria, and all his glory" (Isaiah 8:7).
- **A simile** compares one thing with another: "his eyes were as a flame of
  fire" (Revelation 1:14).

They are read the way scripture's figures are read elsewhere, and two rules govern
them:
- **A symbol stands for another thing because of a quality the two share.**
- **So it stands for that quality, not for one fixed thing.** Iron does not always
  stand for one nation. It stands for something strong, or for something of low
  worth, whichever the context brings forward.

**Whether a passage is figurative is itself a reading to show.** An earthquake in a
vision need not be a literal earthquake. It may be an event like one in its
effects, such as the overthrow of a kingdom. But a figurative reading must be shown
in one of these ways:
- from the passage's own signals: a vision, a stated comparison, or a sense the
  literal reading cannot bear;
- from scripture's use of the same figure elsewhere.

It is never a way to set aside a plain statement the reader finds difficult.
Neither reading is the default. A literal reading that the passage's own signals
rule out is an error too.

1. **Several terms or symbols can stand for one class of thing.** Those who obey
   God's commandments are called the seed of the woman, the righteous, the godly,
   the saints and the children of God. All of these name one class of people, not
   different groups. So do not multiply groups because the terms differ. Equally,
   that two terms name the same class is itself a claim to show, from parallel
   statements, from one being put for the other, or from what is said of each. It
   is not to be assumed from a likeness.
2. **One term or symbol can stand for several things that share one quality of
   it.**
   - **Brass for Greece, because brass is strong.** Daniel 2 does not name its
     third kingdom, but Daniel 8:20–21 names the kingdoms after Babylon as Media
     and Persia, then Grecia. The identification comes from reading the visions
     together, by principle 5 above. Daniel 2:40 states the quality for the kingdom
     after it, "strong as iron". Brass stands for strength elsewhere too
     (Deuteronomy 33:25; Job 6:12; 40:18; 41:27).
   - **Brass for people who are spiritually valueless (Ezekiel 22:18),** because
     brass is common and cheap.
   - **Brass for the flesh, in the brazen serpent of Numbers 21:9,** because of
     its reddish colour.

   Other symbols work the same way. Each pairing below is a symbol, the quality the
   context draws on, and the text that shows it:
   - **The eagle:** speed (Lamentations 4:19) and strength (Isaiah 40:31).
   - **Fire:** what destroys (Psalm 80:16) and what refines (Malachi 3:2).
   - **The yoke:** willing service (Matthew 11:29) and subjection to an enemy
     (Deuteronomy 28:48).

   The fig tree shows how context sets a symbol's polarity as well as its
   quality.
   - **Peace and prosperity.** Under one's vine and fig tree stands for that state
     (1 Kings 4:25; Micah 4:4; Zechariah 3:10).
   - **Their absence.** A fig tree bare or withered stands for loss of that state
     (Jeremiah 8:13; Joel 1:12; Habakkuk 3:17). What the text says of the tree's
     state decides which.
   - **A quick fall.** The fig tree also has another quality: its first-ripe figs
     fall at a shake. So it can stand for a quick and easy fall, as with
     Nineveh's strongholds (Nahum 3:12). Revelation 6:13 takes up the same image,
     the stars falling "as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs, when she is
     shaken of a mighty wind".

   So a symbol's meaning in one passage does not carry over to another by
   itself. Name the quality the symbol shares with what it stands for in this
   context, and show that the context brings that quality forward.

How strongly a meaning is shown:

- **Scripture interprets the symbol in context.** This is the strongest evidence.
  Daniel 2 interprets its image: the head of gold is the king to whom it is spoken.
  A meaning given this way is Stated.
- **Its use elsewhere in scripture.** The next evidence is the symbol's use
  elsewhere, with a quality of the thing that fits this context. That is an
  inference: grade it with `echoes-and-patterns.md`. The writer's own usage counts
  first, as in *Breadth of witness*.
- **A key from outside scripture.** A meaning that needs a key scripture does not
  supply is Imported. That includes a symbol system, a later tradition's lexicon of
  images, or a historical identification the text does not make. Expositions of
  prophecy often print catalogues of symbols. Treat an entry like a cross-reference
  link: a pointer to scripture's uses of the figure, which must be read before the
  meaning is relied on.

## In an audit

Prophecy writing is often confident, and its confidence is the thing to measure.
Report the usual findings:
- a fulfilment stated as declared when it is inferred;
- a symbol's meaning carried from one passage to another without showing the
  shared quality;
- a passage read figuratively without the passage's signals or scripture's use
  of the figure, or literally against them;
- a timeline presented as one text's statement;
- a historical identification presented in scripture's voice.

Do not report a reading merely for proposing a further fulfilment or reading a
symbol from its use elsewhere: both are the method these principles describe. And
do not report an unfulfilled prophecy as failed. By principle 2, check what
scripture says about it, and by principle 3, whether the time it speaks of has
come.
